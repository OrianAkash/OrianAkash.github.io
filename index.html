Ember’s Escape

The game in action: heat, melting and fire puzzles on our custom C++ engine.

A 2D puzzle-platformer on our own C++ engine. You play a flame: melting ice and lighting fires drain the same meter as your health, so every ability is a resource decision.

Role
Team lead, product manager and engine programmer
Team
7 people, 2 trimesters, 6 milestones to gold
Stack
C++, OpenGL, ImGui, FMOD

The problem

The engine couldn’t play video, but the game needed cutscenes. Playtests also showed players running out of heat without noticing it coming.

What I did

  • Built a cutscene player on Windows Media Foundation for video and WASAPI for its audio.
  • Built the low-health warning: a screen vignette plus muffled audio, with thresholds designers can tune without touching code. Try a version of it below.
  • Built the core puzzle objects: meltable ice, food blocks and the heat beam.

The result

The cutscene system, 1,131 lines, shipped in the gold build. In the M2 playtest, 80% of testers rated the UI very highly and every tester scored the audio 4 or 5 out of 5.

How I led

  • Was the main link between art, design and tech, tracked everyone’s progress and kept us on deadline from Unity prototype to gold build.
  • Ran a playtest every milestone and turned the results into changes, like reversible food-block states that remove soft-locks.

Atelier3D

In development this semester

A Scratch-style 3D game-creation tool that teaches teenagers to code, built on our own C++20 engine and editor. It’s our capstone project, and I’m still working on it.

Role
Audio and physics programmer
Team
9 people, two-semester capstone
Stack
C++20, FMOD Core, CMake, clang-format, Jira

The problem

The team wanted FMOD for audio, but FMOD doesn’t support every toolchain we build on. The engine still had to compile and run everywhere.

What I did

  • Wrote one AudioSystem interface with two backends: FMOD Core where it’s available, and a silent fallback everywhere else.
  • Made sound data-driven: JSON sound banks define 3D sound effects and music, routed through mixer buses.
  • Built the physics side of the engine as well, so I own both audio and physics in the app.

The result

About 1,700 lines shipped in Milestone 1, following the team’s CMake, clang-format and Jira workflow.

03 — 03

GOAP Outpost Defense

A research demo of goal-oriented action planning. Three NPC agents defend an outpost and nothing is scripted: each one picks a goal, and a regressive A* planner finds the cheapest sequence of actions to reach it.

Role
Main programmer: planner, simulation and app
Team
4 people, AI research project
Stack
C++17, SFML, Visual Studio

What I built

  • The whole planning side: world-state facts, actions, the regressive A* planner, the simulation, the contract header and the planner unit tests. About 1,600 lines in one milestone.
  • The SFML app on top: window, rendering, input, and the labels that show each agent’s goal and plan. About 600 lines.
  • In total about 2,000 of the project’s 2,500 lines of code, according to the commit history.

How it works

  • Before planning, the live simulation is compressed into 9 true/false facts, such as HasWeapon or LowHealth. The planner never sees a coordinate, which keeps search fast and easy to reason about.
  • Nine actions, each with preconditions, effects and a cost. Goals run in priority order: survive, call for help, defend, patrol. The highest one that applies wins, re-checked every tick.
  • The simulation is pure C++17 with no graphics. A single contract header separates it from the SFML renderer, so the planner can be unit-tested and run headless.

Try it

  • Change the world while it runs: spawn threats, drop weapons, injure an agent or make melee cheap. The agents replan on the spot, and each one shows its current goal and plan above its head.

About

I’m Akash. I like the low-level work that decides how a game feels: when a sound plays, how a frame reaches the screen, what an agent decides to do next.

I’ve led a seven-person team to a gold build while writing engine code, and this semester I’ve built the audio and physics for a nine-person C++20 engine. Leading taught me to explain technical trade-offs to artists and designers, and to plan work so it lands on the milestone.

Before university I wrote lessons on AI, drones and micro:bit programming for primary and secondary students, and taught coding programmes in schools. Breaking hard topics into small steps is still how I approach unfamiliar code. I also study psychology, which shapes how I think about what players notice and feel.
